\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:baselines}

We evaluate four questions: whether response-guided resampling produces detectable signal;
how retry budget and plausibility filtering affect measured quality; how ReTrace compares
with token-based baselines at their actual compute budgets; and how quickly the signal
degrades after editing.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

All experiments use \texttt{GSAI-ML/LLaDA-8B-Instruct} in fp16 on one TITAN RTX. Prompts
come from C4 \texttt{realnewslike} and are truncated to 300 characters, following the
dgMARK evaluation protocol. Unless noted otherwise, the model generates 512 tokens in
32-token blocks with the number of denoising steps equal to the generation length. ReTrace
uses eight ablation patterns, $s_{\min}=0.5$, full-vocabulary sampling at temperature
$0.8$, and a per-document HMAC-derived key. The provenance experiments assign every
carrier to the presence pool; they do not evaluate payload recovery.

Quality is GPT-2-large perplexity (PPL). For ReTrace, every watermarked document is paired
with a control generated by the same scheduler with steering disabled. We report
$\exp(\operatorname{mean}(\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{wm}}-
\mathrm{NLL}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}))$, so a ratio below one means that the external PPL model
preferred the watermarked sample on average. It does not by itself establish better
coherence or task performance. Documents shorter than the stated validity threshold after
decoding are excluded from paired PPL, and the remaining count is reported. Detection uses
the exact binomial tail from Eq.~\eqref{eq:test}; TPR is measured at fixed analytic FPR
thresholds.

Compute is reported in \emph{sequence evaluations}. A 512-token reference decode uses 512
evaluations. ReTrace adds nine masked sequences per block---eight ablations and one
unablated conditional---for 144 additional evaluations during generation and another 144
at verification. The ablations are batched in the implementation, so sequence evaluations
are hardware-independent but are not the same as wall-clock model invocations.

\paragraph{Retries strengthen the signal but can select unlikely tokens.}
On 30 held-out 512-token prompts, increasing $R$ from 2 to 4 to 8 raises TPR at $1\%$ FPR
from $0.73$ to $0.83$ to $0.87$ (Figure~\ref{fig:operating}b). No extra DLM evaluation is
needed because all retries reuse the current probability row. The corresponding PPL
ratios, however, rise from $1.14\times$ to $1.40\times$ and $1.61\times$. This trend is
consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:dist}: additional attempts make acceptance more likely, but
they also increase the chance of selecting a lower-probability member of the acceptance
set.

At $R=1$, a disjoint 40-document run obtains $0.38$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR and a
$0.98\times$ paired PPL ratio, although only 17 pairs pass the decoded-length criterion.
Length provides another source of power: at $R=1$, increasing the continuation from 256 to
512 tokens raises TPR at $1\%$ FPR from $0.25$ to $0.40$ by increasing the number of
carriers.

\paragraph{The plausibility floor controls measured PPL cost.}
With $R=8$ and $\kappa=0.1$, 30 held-out 512-token documents reach $0.70$ TPR at $1\%$
FPR and a $0.87\times$ PPL ratio. The mean sign-match rate falls from $0.711$ without the
floor to $0.620$, while the matched control remains at $0.501$. Decoded bigram repetition
is $0.33$ for ReTrace and $0.36$ for its control under the shared validity rule. A smaller
floor with more retries ($R=16,\kappa=0.05$) reaches $0.80$ TPR and a $0.74\times$ ratio
on 20 documents, with 15 valid PPL pairs.

Ratios below one are expected to be possible because the floor explicitly favors tokens
with high probability under the live DLM conditional, which can also be rewarded by
GPT-2-large. We therefore interpret these numbers as ``no PPL penalty detected'' rather
than as evidence that the watermark improves text. At 1024 tokens, $R=8,\kappa=0.1$
reaches $0.75$ TPR at $1\%$ and $0.69$ at $0.1\%$, with a $0.83\times$ PPL ratio. This
longer setting is less conclusive: only 8 of 16 pairs meet the quality-validity rule, and
bigram repetition increases from $0.565$ in the control to $0.623$.

\subsection{Comparison with token-based baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} separates methods by generation budget. At one decode, KGW
reaches $0.93$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR with a $1.03\times$ ratio, but requires strictly
left-to-right DLM generation. The local dgMARK reproduction reaches $0.74$ at
$1.55\times$ with one candidate and $0.86$ at $1.23\times$ with three-beam lookahead. The
latter uses roughly four sequence evaluations per generated token.

ReTrace's 512-token decode costs $656/512=1.28\times$ as many sequence evaluations as the
reference sampler. Its best measured PPL settings reach $0.70$--$0.80$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR,
below both KGW and three-beam dgMARK. ReTrace's distinguishing result is therefore not
state-of-the-art detection. It is the existence of a usable operating point for a
model-response statistic with an exact conditional null. The cost is substantial at
verification: 144 masked sequence evaluations, compared with zero model evaluations for
KGW and dgMARK.

The baseline comparison also has protocol qualifications. KGW is evaluated with distinct
bigrams because repeated bigrams share the same green-list event; without deduplication,
the unwatermarked left-to-right control measured a $37\%$ false-positive rate. Strict
left-to-right LLaDA also has a $1.17\times$ PPL cost relative to block decoding and high
repetition. Conversely, the local dgMARK reproduction is weaker than the result reported
in its source paper. The table is a same-machine reproduction, not a claim that these rows
are definitive performance ceilings for either baseline.

\subsection{Detector checks and ablations}

The exact-null claim in Proposition~\ref{prop:null} is analytical. Current ReTrace control
arms provide only a finite-sample sanity check: the 30-document retry experiment and the
40-document $R=1$ confirmation each produce zero detections at the $1\%$ threshold. These
samples are too small to estimate a $1\%$ false-positive rate precisely, and we do not use
them as a substitute for the theorem.

On identical cached 512-token outputs, a gated sign count, a gated likelihood-ratio score,
and an all-position likelihood-ratio score each reach $0.38$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR for $R=1$.
Counting all positions without the two-sided gate falls to $0.33$. Since the likelihood
scores add complexity without measured gain at this operating point, the reported ReTrace
detector uses the gated binomial count.

\subsection{Post-editing robustness}

The response signal is fragile to edits. On 20 $R=1$ documents, same-model argmax
re-denoising of a random $10\%$ of positions reduces TPR at $1\%$ FPR from $0.35$ to
$0.05$; deleting $10\%$ of tokens has the same measured effect. Attacked control arms
remain near the nominal threshold. The failure is consistent with the construction:
challenges in a later block condition on the completed prefix, so one early edit can alter
many downstream contrasts.

This experiment rules out post-edit robustness as a current strength of ReTrace.
Token-local schemes retain an important advantage when text is likely to be paraphrased,
deleted, or re-denoised. Content-anchored challenges or an edit-synchronization layer may
help, but neither is implemented or evaluated here.

\subsection{Limitations}

The evaluation uses one DLM checkpoint, one corpus, and small confirmatory samples
($n=16$--$40$ for the principal ReTrace rows). PPL and bigram repetition do not fully
measure semantic quality, and the longest generations have substantial validity attrition.
ReTrace also requires the verifier to possess the exact model checkpoint and to run it
hundreds of times per document. Finally, carrier supply depends on conditional entropy:
sharp distributions offer few positions with mass on both response signs. These limitations
leave cross-model generality, downstream-task quality, and scalable verification open.
