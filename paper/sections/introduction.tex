\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Existing language-model watermarks encode provenance in \emph{what tokens are produced}.
Red--green schemes bias the token distribution toward a keyed vocabulary
partition~\citep{kirchenbauer2023watermark}; distortion-free schemes fix the sampling
randomness with a keyed pseudorandom sequence; and the watermarks designed for diffusion
language models exploit the extra freedom those models expose --- unresolved context,
per-step sampling randomness, global sequence statistics, or the order in which positions
are unmasked~\citep{hong2026dgmark}. Different as they are, every one of them leaves the
verifier reading a \emph{surface signature}: a hash of token identities, a frequency, a
rank. We ask a different question.

\important{Can a watermark be detected from how the model reconstructs a text, rather than
from the tokens the text contains?}

A diffusion language model makes this question answerable in a way an autoregressive model
does not. Its training objective is to recover masked tokens from arbitrary surviving
context, so any finished text can be pushed \emph{back} through the operator that produced
it: corrupt it, re-denoise it, and observe the response. That response is a property of the
text's relationship to the model, not of its symbols, and it is available to anyone holding
the model --- but only interpretable by someone holding the key that chose the corruption.

We call the resulting scheme \textbf{ReTrace}, and its verifier is easiest to describe as
a keyed question posed to every token of the text. The key hides two different parts of
the text's own context --- say, the sentences nearest a span in one version and the
farthest in the other --- and the model scores the span's tokens under both corruptions.
Each token then \emph{answers} the question ``which context do you rely on more?'': the
sign of
\begin{equation}
g_i(y_i) \;=\; \log p_\theta\!\left(y_i \mid C^1_i(y)\right) - \log p_\theta\!\left(y_i \mid C^0_i(y)\right).
\label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
The key also fixes, per position, which answer a watermarked text \emph{should} give, and
the verifier counts agreements. A text written without the key cannot know the questions,
so each of its answers matches an independent hidden coin: agreements follow an exact
$\mathrm{Binomial}(n,\tfrac12)$ law, and provenance becomes an ordinary hypothesis test.
No vocabulary partition, no token hash --- the evidence lives in the model's behaviour.

How does the watermark get the answers \emph{into} the text? The failed way first,
because it shaped the design (\S\ref{sec:v1}): editing tokens after generation. On a
well-trained denoiser the conditionals are sharp --- at the median position exactly one
token is fluent enough to use, so there is nothing to edit toward. Worse, the effect
\emph{strengthens} as generation improves: a sampler repair that lowered draft perplexity
from $22.2$ to $9.5$ also shrank the set of usable alternatives by $34\text{--}60\,\%$.
\important{Better diffusion-model generation reduces post-hoc watermarkability}, and it
rules out an entire family of designs.

What survives is to never override the model at all. ReTrace decodes exactly as the base
model would, with one added rule: at positions where the keyed question is genuinely
open --- both answers plausible, which the key can determine on its own --- the model's
sampled token is checked against the desired answer, and \emph{resampled from the same
distribution} (at most $R$ times, at no extra forward pass) until an agreeing token comes
up; if none does, the natural sample is kept. Every committed token is therefore a sample
the model itself proposed. A key-free \emph{plausibility floor} $p(v) \geq \kappa\,
p_{\max}$ additionally refuses agreeing-but-unlikely tokens, capping what any single
position can cost. The measured effect of both guards together is that watermarked
perplexity sits \emph{at or below} the matched non-watermarked control
($0.74\times$--$0.87\times$) at $\mathrm{TPR}@1\%$ of $0.70$--$0.80$ on $512$-token
documents, with the whole embedder costing $1.28\times$ a plain decode.

\important{ReTrace places the watermark in resampled tokens and reads it back from the
model's reconstruction response} (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

In sum: a watermark read from the model's response rather than its tokens, verifiable
from text, model and key alone (\S\ref{sec:method}); a counting statistic with a
finite-sample exact binomial null under the ideal-PRF model, false-positive control
verified at the \emph{worst} of twenty keys (\S\ref{sec:theory}); an embedding that never
leaves the model's conditional and holds quality at or below its control
(\S\ref{sec:embedding}); a mechanism-level negative result --- substitution is priced
out, and better generation makes it worse (\S\ref{sec:v1}); and an evaluation that
reports what each method pays, not only what it scores --- generation budget and
detection cost per row, with the advantages \emph{and} the limitations of the design
stated explicitly against them (\S\ref{sec:baselines}).
