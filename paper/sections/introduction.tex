\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\input{figures/fig_paradigm}

Language-model text can be difficult for readers to distinguish from human writing, creating
provenance risks in settings that include disinformation, phishing, and academic-integrity
review~\citep{liang2026survey,krishna2023paraphrasing}. Post-hoc detectors are fitted to a
generation process, so their operating characteristics can change when the decoding mechanism
changes, including for masked diffusion language models. Watermarking instead embeds a keyed
statistical signal during generation and allows a key holder to test a later
document~\citep{petitcolas1999hiding,kirchenbauer2023watermark}. Because a false positive can
misattribute human-written text, deployment requires a specified false-positive rate rather than
one inferred only from a calibration corpus. Since
adversaries also paraphrase output and learn a key's behaviour from detector
queries~\citep{krishna2023paraphrasing,jovanovic2024stealing}, we study finite-sample control under
adaptively selected trials~\citep{zhao2024provable,christ2024undetectable}.

Existing watermarks place their secret in the tokens, whether by biasing keyed vocabulary
subsets~\citep{kirchenbauer2023watermark}, by coupling each draw to
pseudorandomness~\citep{kuditipudi2024distortionfree}, or, in recent diffusion language model
(DLM) methods, by exploiting unresolved context and denoising order~\citep{hong2026dgmark}.
Detection then reduces to inspecting the text together with the key, and the generator is never
consulted. Masked DLMs, by contrast, permit a different arrangement,
since a finished text can be fed back to the model with selected context hidden and the
likelihood of an emitted token then moves by an amount the checkpoint alone determines. Hiding a
keyed subset of the context is in this sense a \emph{context probe}, the direction in which the
likelihood moves is the model's response to it, and agreement between the keyed sign and that
response defines the provenance statistic. We apply a probe where the masked-position conditional assigns nonzero mass to
multiple plausible tokens whose responses to two patterns have opposite signs.

Moving the keyed signal from token identities to this relation introduces two requirements absent
from token-local detection: the verifier must reproduce the embedder's probe, and carrier selection
must be completed before the accepted answer is known. A gate with access to the rewarded sign would
otherwise inflate the false-positive rate above the level the detector reports.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} states both as requirements, and existing DLM watermarks avoid them by
keeping detection token-local, whether by removing prefix dependence~\citep{chen2025orderagnostic}, by averaging a
red--green bias over unresolved context~\citep{gloaguen2026watermarking}, or by steering which
mask is resolved next~\citep{hong2026dgmark}, none of which asks the model anything about the
text it produced.

Shibboleth instead meets each condition with one mechanism (Figure~\ref{fig:paradigm}). It settles
replayability by re-masking the current block together with its suffix before scoring the probe
family, and the state it measures therefore follows from the final text alone. A \emph{balanced gate} then admits as carriers only the unresolved positions whose block-masked
conditional retains mass on both response signs, scoring them in a way that is invariant to
swapping the two patterns and therefore blind to the sign the key will ask for.
For generation, Shibboleth draws at most $R$ proposals from the
unchanged live row at an admitted position and commits the first one carrying that sign, which
amounts to rejection sampling and does change the output distribution, although every committed
token remains one the model proposed. Verification afterwards re-applies the same probe,
reapplies the gate, and returns an exact binomial tail. Unlike a prefix-based watermark, which can require strict left-to-right decoding, and unlike an
order-guided one, which changes which mask is resolved next, Shibboleth acts only after the
reference schedule has produced a row, and therefore leaves the block partition and the
confidence order untouched.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Across 36 post-hoc substitution settings, the highest-TPR configuration within a $1.35\times$
perplexity budget reaches $0.10$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR; we therefore embed while the conditional is
available during generation (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}).
\item We propose Shibboleth, in which block-and-suffix re-masking makes the probe replayable from
the finished text and orientation-blind gating keeps the accepted answer independent of how the
carriers were discovered, which together keep the detector's $p$-values exact without a
calibration corpus (Sections~\ref{sec:observable} to~\ref{sec:theory}).
\item We evaluate Shibboleth on LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-7B-Instruct and report both sides
of the trade: $R=16,\kappa=0.05$ reaches $0.80$ and $0.90$ TPR at $1\%$ FPR — on
LLaDA with the lowest perplexity ratio among the compared rows, and on Dream with the highest
TPR under shared settings — whereas verification uses 144 masked evaluations
and $10\%$ re-denoising reduces TPR from $0.35$ to $0.05$ at $R=1$ and from $0.88$ to $0.50$ at $R=16$
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}
