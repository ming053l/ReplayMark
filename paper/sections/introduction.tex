\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Existing language-model watermarks encode provenance in \emph{what tokens are produced}.
Red--green schemes bias the token distribution toward a keyed vocabulary
partition~\citep{kirchenbauer2023watermark}; distortion-free schemes fix the sampling
randomness with a keyed pseudorandom sequence; and the watermarks designed for diffusion
language models exploit the extra freedom those models expose --- unresolved context,
per-step sampling randomness, global sequence statistics, or the order in which positions
are unmasked~\citep{hong2026dgmark}. Different as they are, every one of them leaves the
verifier reading a \emph{surface signature}: a hash of token identities, a frequency, a
rank. We ask a different question.

\important{Can provenance be encoded in how a diffusion language model \emph{reconstructs}
a text, rather than in which tokens the text contains?}

A diffusion language model makes this question answerable in a way an autoregressive model
does not. Its training objective is to recover masked tokens from arbitrary surviving
context, so any finished text can be pushed \emph{back} through the operator that produced
it: corrupt it, re-denoise it, and observe the response. That response is a property of the
text's relationship to the model, not of its symbols, and it is available to anyone holding
the model --- but only interpretable by someone holding the key that chose the corruption.

We call the resulting scheme \textbf{BasinMark}. A secret key derives two corruption
patterns over the context of a span. Reading the model's log-probability of each
\emph{observed} token under both corruptions gives a contrast
\begin{equation}
g_i(y_i) \;=\; \log p_\theta\!\left(y_i \mid C^1_i(y)\right) - \log p_\theta\!\left(y_i \mid C^0_i(y)\right),
\label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
and the watermark asks not that $y_i$ belong to a keyed vocabulary subset, but that the
sign of $g_i(y_i)$ agree with a keyed orientation. The evidence lives in the model's
behaviour.

Getting the embedding right took one full negative result, which we report because it is
the reason the final design looks the way it does. The obvious way to place such a
watermark is to \emph{substitute} tokens: at keyed positions, replace the model's choice
with an admissible alternative whose contrast points the right way. On a well-trained
diffusion language model this does not work, and the reason is a property of the model
rather than of the algorithm. Under reference decoding the denoiser's conditionals are
sharp enough that the median position admits exactly one token inside any usable fluency
budget, so every unit of watermark evidence must be bought with a unit of fluency. Worse,
the effect strengthens as generation improves: repairing a sampler bug in our own harness
lowered draft perplexity from $22.2$ to $9.5$ and simultaneously halved the denoiser's
entropy and shrank the admissible set by $34\text{--}60\,\%$. We state this as a finding in
its own right --- \important{better diffusion-model generation reduces post-hoc
watermarkability} --- and it rules out an entire family of designs.

What survives is to stop changing \emph{which} token is produced and change only
\emph{when} a model-preferred token is committed. Decoding a block, the model proposes what
it would have proposed anyway; a position whose proposal already answers the keyed
challenge is committed first, and one that does not is deferred. No token is ever
overridden, which is why this costs almost nothing --- the same reason decoding-order
watermarks are cheap~\citep{hong2026dgmark}. The difference is what the verifier reads:
order is only the \emph{embedding} channel here, while the evidence recovered at
verification time is the model's keyed reconstruction behaviour.

\important{BasinMark embeds through the denoising trajectory but verifies through the
model's keyed reconstruction behaviour.}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A provenance signal that is behavioural rather than lexical: the response of a
finished text to keyed re-denoising, readable from the text, the model and the key alone,
with no access to the generation trajectory (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item A test statistic that is a sum of bounded indicators with one keyed orientation bit
per position, giving a finite-sample exact $\mathrm{Binomial}(n,\tfrac12)$ null under the
ideal-PRF randomization model, with presence, claimed-message verification and payload
decoding kept separate (\S\ref{sec:theory}). False-positive control is verified per key and
at the worst key, not averaged over keys.
\item An embedding rule that never substitutes a token, steering only the commit order
inside each block of the reference decoding schedule (\S\ref{sec:embedding}).
\item A negative result with a mechanism: post-hoc substitution is priced out on modern
diffusion language models, and improving generation quality makes it worse
(\S\ref{sec:v1}).
\end{itemize}
